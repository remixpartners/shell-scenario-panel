# There Is Another Way to Bring AI to Your City

*Draft op-ed for discussion. Co-authored: Shaina Doar (Onward Ventures) and Justin Massa (Remix Partners).*

*Working headline options:*
*1. There Is Another Way to Bring AI to Your City*
*2. AI Will Reshape Your City's Economy. Who Captures the Value Is Still a Choice.*
*3. The AI Question Your City Isn't Being Asked*

*Dek: The debate over AI in the public sector has collapsed into two bad options. Leaders have a third, and the window to choose it is closing.*

---

Ask almost any mayor, governor, or economic development director about artificial intelligence today, and you will hear one of two answers. The first is about regulation: which task force, which guardrails, which risks to contain. The second is about procurement: which platform to standardize on, which vendor to trust, which "family" to join.

Both answers are reasonable. Both are also incomplete, and it is worth asking why. The people supplying the dominant story about AI are, for the most part, the people selling it. They are not lying. But they have a financial stake in your conclusion, and that stake quietly shapes the questions you are encouraged to ask. "How do we adopt this safely?" is a very different question from "who captures the value this creates?" The first keeps you a customer. The second makes you a strategist.

The second question is the one that matters, because AI is going to create real economic value, and value always accrues to someone. Right now, an enormous amount of money is being spent to make sure it accrues to the platforms rather than to your community. The deals being struck are designed to turn what should be a swappable commodity into something with high switching costs. The result is predictable. Value created inside your city's businesses flows out of your city.

Consider the paradox sitting in plain sight. The investment pouring into AI is aimed almost entirely at large enterprises. And when you ask large employers what AI will do to their workforce, the bigger they are, the more likely they are to say it will let them cut jobs. Now ask small businesses the same question. According to a 2025 report from the U.S. Small Business Administration's Office of Advocacy, businesses with fewer than twenty employees are the only ones that, on balance, expect AI to grow their headcount rather than shrink it. They are also the ones being ignored. The same study found that 82 percent of the smallest firms say AI is simply "not applicable" to them.

Read those two facts together and the stakes for any city come into focus. Small and mid-sized businesses are roughly half of local employment. They are the economic base. They are also the segment most likely to add jobs because of AI, and the segment receiving almost none of the help. Meanwhile, the large employers who are receiving the help expect to use it to employ fewer people. Left on its current track, AI hands your biggest companies a tool to shrink their payrolls, which erodes your tax base, while the businesses that would actually hire are left to figure it out alone. The city absorbs the cost and misses the upside.

None of this is inevitable. It is the product of choices, and choices can be made differently.

This is why "regulate it" misses the point. Regulation is reactive. It tells you what to prevent. It does not tell you what kind of economy you are trying to build. The more useful question for a public leader is not how to referee AI but how to harness it. Given our industry mix, where are we vulnerable and where can we grow? Given our residents' skills, where do we need to invest in training, from kindergarten through adults already in the workforce? What happens to our downtown if professional-services offices empty out, and how do we get ahead of that rather than scramble after it? These are economic development questions. They have been the work of cities for a century. AI does not change the job. It raises the stakes.

The encouraging part is that the tools to act well are now within reach, and not only for the wealthiest places. The cost of capable AI is falling even as its capability climbs. A public library could soon offer the kind of capability that today sits behind a corporate subscription, for little more than the cost of the electricity to run it. In most governments the real bottleneck is procurement, not technology, and procurement is something leaders can fix. The cities that move deliberately, that keep their options open instead of locking themselves to one provider, that treat AI as a question of local prosperity rather than a compliance exercise, will capture value their neighbors let slip away.

What this moment calls for is not another task force and not another platform. It is an alternative: a way of bringing AI into civic life that is evidence-based instead of vendor-driven, that keeps value local instead of exporting it, and that starts from the interests of residents and small businesses rather than the firms with the biggest marketing budgets. This is not anti-innovation. It is the opposite. It is a bet that communities, given the full picture and a little help, will make better decisions for themselves than anyone will make for them.

The story your city is being told about AI is not wrong, exactly. It is incomplete, told by people with a reason to leave parts out. The parts they leave out are the ones that decide who benefits. There is another way to do this, and we think it is the one worth choosing. The only real mistake would be to assume there isn't, and let the choice get made for you.
