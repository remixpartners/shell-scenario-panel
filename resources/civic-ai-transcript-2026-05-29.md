## Summary
### Family/school transition planning (Minneapolis → Chicago)
- The family expects to remain in Minneapolis for one more year because the children are enrolled for next school year (current grades referenced: 7th, 5th, 2nd).
- They are considering moving when the oldest starts 9th grade in Chicago to ease the high-school transition.
- The middle child’s move into middle school was described as the hardest adjustment; the youngest adapts easily.
### School technology posture and AI readiness (Saint Ignatius)
- [Speaker 2] visited/assessed Saint Ignatius and was impressed with how the school addressed technology and AI questions.
- Saint Ignatius reportedly uses iPads rather than laptops; participants found this unusual and potentially limiting for some AI use cases.
- [Speaker 2] valued the school’s acknowledgement that AI/tech is evolving quickly and their posture may change by next year.
- A larger meeting with Saint Ignatius was mentioned as happening “in like two weeks,” but no date/agenda was confirmed.
### Community tension on tech in schools; need for a “CTO strategist”
- [Speaker 2] described conflict with parents advocating for “no technology in school.”
- They argued schools should engage proactively because technology/AI is unavoidable, and this is not just an IT manager’s job.
- Proposed role: a CTO-like strategist who sets a clear vision for families, communicates it, and revisits it quarterly due to rapid change.
### AI education and program concepts
- Justin referenced the “S2G” group and Sanjeev’s repeated interest in funding an “AI swim school” concept (weekend AI education for kids) if a viable business concept is developed.
- A Saint Ignatius board member’s connection to the US Association of Jesuit High Schools could provide a distribution pathway if an MVP performs well.
- Concept direction: pilot at Saint Ignatius as an MVP, with the longer-term goal of an independent AI training offering for high schoolers (not relying on K–12 as the primary channel).
- A “third lane” was added: how schools should embrace AI and what new roles and operating models are required.
### Advisor connection
- [Speaker 2] suggested speaking with “Tom’s dad,” a long-time head of school at North Shore Country Day School who still advises other heads of school; Justin expressed interest.
### Public sentiment, organizational friction, and trust parallels
- Justin observed public attitudes toward generative AI declining sharply (especially Gen Z/Gen Alpha) while executives remain enthusiastic—often creating internal misalignment, resistance, and “therapy” work.
- [Speaker 2] connected this to earlier trust shifts in big tech (e.g., post–Cambridge Analytica) and argued there is “a way through this.”
- They discussed the need for credible intermediaries (e.g., universities/labs) to address backlash while still articulating citizen and growth benefits.
### Market dynamics: enterprise focus vs small business needs; Chicago ecosystem
- Justin described major funding/partnership momentum around enterprise deployments (e.g., OpenAI/Anthropic with consulting/private equity), with incentives that can prioritize “selling tokens.”
- He argued small businesses may benefit more from today’s AI but receive far less support, despite being a major share of city employment and the economic base.
- Example: a P33 Chicago training for ~100 small businesses involving Anthropic and a non-local AI consulting firm; frustration that local firms weren’t used.
- [Speaker 2] offered to introduce Justin to P33 contacts; John Ramirez and “Desiree” were mentioned but not resolved.
### Vendor-neutral AI services model: General Catalyst / Percepta
- [Speaker 2] researched General Catalyst and Percepta; GC’s CEO’s Cityblock/Sidewalk Labs connection was noted as relevant context.
- General Catalyst reportedly acquired a health system in Ohio to operationalize (“full stack”) its technology investments.
- Percepta was described as a “neutral” BPO-like services model that began by scouting portfolio opportunities and expanded to serve external companies.
- A claimed partnership with the State of Maryland and Anthropic was referenced, including a recollection of “$500,000,” but details were not verified.
- Rationale: a neutral party can evaluate options without locking public agencies into a single vendor (vs Accenture-style engagements).
### AI adoption architecture (“the stack”) and lock-in risk
- Justin shared a stack framing (from a slide deck dated 2026-05-29): model selection, a “harness,” integrated tools/connectors, skills to enable actions, people/humans, and company files/data connectivity.
- He emphasized parts of the stack should remain commoditized/swappable; vendor strategies and partner incentives often create switching costs and bias toward a single model provider.
### Civic/economic development narrative: who captures AI value
- Justin proposed a civic framing: AI will create value, but the key question is who captures it—platforms or local businesses.
- Concern: adoption patterns may impose new costs without net local economic benefit.
- [Speaker 2] noted leaders may default to choosing a “family” (Microsoft vs Google) despite fast-moving model quality; Justin argued modular architectures can make switching trivial (e.g., ~15 minutes), but most orgs aren’t built that way.
### SMB data points and workforce implications (to validate)
- Justin cited market-sizing findings: very low SMB usage and limited near-term adoption intent; “82%” of very small businesses reportedly say gen AI is “not applicable.”
- He also cited a split expectation: smaller businesses more likely to believe AI will increase hiring; larger businesses more likely to expect job cuts.
- Risk framing for municipal leaders: large firms get help adopting AI and may reduce headcount (tax-base risk), while SMBs—community “lifeblood”—could grow but aren’t being supported.
### City strategy themes beyond regulation (20-year view)
- [Speaker 2] emphasized moving beyond “how do we regulate” toward shaping what kind of city they want over the next 20 years, including an AI-ready industry mix and SMB ecosystem.
- Education/workforce idea: a CPS “chief AI strategist” and broader resident readiness “from K–12 all the way through.”
### Real estate and the Loop risk
- [Speaker 2] predicted a potential “hollowing out of the loop again” within ~3 years, with implications for the tax base, and noted insufficient public discussion.
### Infrastructure and energy/compute messaging (and myth-dispelling)
- Topics raised: self-driving transportation shifts; data centers and energy demand.
- Justin pushed for targeted subtopics rather than an all-encompassing infrastructure plan and advocated “myth dispelling,” especially challenging prevailing narratives about water usage.
- He warned electricity impacts may become more visible as subsidies fade, and raised concerns about “behind-the-meter” gas turbines being harmful and undesirable near residents.
- [Speaker 2] expressed interest in distributed/smaller data centers near critical services (e.g., hospitals) to ensure critical infrastructure reliability.
### Civic infrastructure: libraries and community-owned compute
- Justin referenced John Bracken (DPLA) working on a library AI strategy supported by a grant; Justin plans to join their advisory board.
- He proposed “libraries should own compute” (community-owned compute), anticipating older-model capabilities becoming cheap enough that access and electricity + local hardware could deliver high practical value for most tasks.
### Compute pricing/shortages and subsidy dynamics
- Justin cited an OpenAI message about pre-buying compute due to expected shortages starting “this year.”
- He claimed consumer AI accounts are subsidized at roughly one-tenth of real cost and likened the trajectory to Uber-style subsidize-then-raise pricing once users are locked in.
### Government operations and procurement as core blockers
- Participants noted many cities are stuck on limited “Copilot”-style implementations and slowed by procurement; choosing “the right tool” was framed as a leadership/business decision.
- Procurement reform was positioned as a major barrier and potential deliverable area, analogous to the shift from on-prem to SaaS.
### Proposed offering: diagnostic + initial tools + sprints/ongoing capability
- A framework engagement was discussed: a diagnostic/report covering AI’s economic impacts, infrastructure, governance, and a “full diagnostic” of operational opportunities.
- Provide an initial “taste” via an initial set of “three tools.”
- Potential ongoing model: sprints as the core delivery, possible BPO-style capability, and structured innovation events (hackathons/new versions of them).
### Publication, credibility, and “alternative culture” positioning
- Open question: whether the main report should be public or internal-only; public versions may require an institutional credibility partner (e.g., Brookings).
- An “all-star” advisor bench was seen as a differentiator to position the team as a McKinsey/IDEO-level alternative.
- They discussed building a senior-leader community (forums/newsletters/podcasts; even a paid private Discord) for leaders navigating uncertainty.
- They emphasized an “alternative culture” to AI adoption (explicitly not “bro branding”), avoiding framing as a DEI/women-led pitch while highlighting conflicts of interest in dominant vendor narratives.
### Timeline, go-to-market, and funding strategy
- Capacity constraints were noted (team “sold out through July 2026”); plan was to refine and pitch during summer 2026, with a possible kickoff in August 2026, likely sliding into fall; a 3–4 month intensive engagement model was suggested.
- Outreach approach: create a short deck/website emphasizing credentials and the “alternative path,” and pitch a defined list (e.g., 10) of state economic development / civic committee leaders.
- Test contact mentioned: Christy Dula (Deputy Director, Illinois DCEO), as an initial conversation even if ultimate targets are more senior.
- Funding/partner model: identify foundations/partners that can fund multi-city work and reduce procurement friction; examples referenced include Ford (as a model for funding work with Maryland) and MacArthur; ambition stated as multi-city, multi-million (e.g., $5M for five cities) rather than small pilots.
