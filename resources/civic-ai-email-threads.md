Here's a response based on my prompt:

Happy to dig into this — it's a good instinct, because the defensive frame
has a structural problem baked in: it casts government as something AI is
happening *to*. Both of the current questions ("what can we extract from
data centers" and "how do we regulate AI") position the mayor or governor
as a host or a referee — reactive, managing a threat someone else created.
That's a weak posture politically and substantively, and it caps the upside
at "we got a decent deal and avoided the worst harms." You can't win a
future you're only defending against.

The central reframe is a shift in subject: from *AI is being done to us* to *we
are doing something with AI for our people*. Government moves from
host/referee to builder/deployer. Everything below is a way to
operationalize that.

A few load-bearing pillars:

*Compute as productive infrastructure, not a tax piñata.* The defensive
data-center frame treats a facility like a landfill — undesirable but
lucrative, so you negotiate concessions. The trap is that the typical data
center delivers ~30–50 permanent jobs and a giant electric bill. The
opportunity question is different: does this infrastructure let *our*
people and businesses do things they couldn't before? Does it anchor a
research cluster, give local startups and universities cheap compute,
attract the talent that builds companies here? The reframe is from "what
concessions can we squeeze" to "how do we make this compute generative for
the regional economy." If a deal doesn't do that, the offensive frame says
don't take it — not because it's harmful, but because it's not *enough*.

*AI as the tool that makes government visibly work.* This is the most
underused and most winnable frame. Government is itself one of the largest
potential beneficiaries of AI: permitting that takes six months taking six
days, benefits backlogs cleared, 311 that actually resolves things,
infrastructure maintenance predicted before it fails. A mayor who can stand
up and say "this is the best-run city in America and AI is part of why" has
a concrete, ownable win that residents feel directly. This flips AI from a
thing to regulate into a thing to govern *with* — and it's bipartisan,
because everyone likes a government that works.

*Make your workers the ones who wield AI — not the ones it happens to.* The
defensive workforce frame is displacement and retraining-as-damage-control.
The offensive version: AI is a productivity multiplier, and the regions
that win are the ones whose nurses, electricians, small manufacturers, and
shop owners adopt it fastest. This is "AI for the many" — diffusion across
the existing economy — and it's far more politically durable than landing
one big tech campus, because the benefit is broadly distributed rather than
concentrated in a few thousand engineers. The line is: we are not going to
be a place where AI replaces people; we're going to be a place where our
people, armed with AI, out-compete everyone.

*AI-enable your existing industries rather than chasing Silicon Valley.*
Most regions will never be San Francisco, and a narrative that pretends
otherwise dies on contact. But every region has a base — agriculture,
logistics, healthcare, energy, manufacturing — that AI can transform. The
win isn't becoming an AI-*production* hub; it's becoming the national
leader in AI-*applied-to-what-you-already-do*. This plays to real assets
and is achievable, which makes it credible.

*Energy abundance as the forcing function.* Here's a genuinely fresh angle:
AI's enormous power demand, usually framed as a liability, can be the
political cover to finally build the energy capacity, transmission, and
clean generation a region has wanted for years and couldn't get done. "AI's
hunger for power is the best thing to happen to our grid" — the demand pays
for buildout that benefits every ratepayer and every other industry. That
converts the single biggest defensive grievance (data centers strain our
grid and water) into the engine of the opportunity story.

Now, the credibility problem you have to solve for this to land: the
defensive concerns are *legitimate*. Data centers really do consume water
and power and create few jobs; AI really does displace some workers. A
purely sunny "opportunity" narrative that waves those away reads as
captured-by-industry and gets shredded politically. The sophisticated move
is not to deny the downsides but to *fold them into the terms of the
opportunity*. You're not abandoning leverage — you're redirecting it.
"Because we're a place AI investment wants to be, we'll use that leverage
to demand it leaves our grid cleaner, our workers more skilled, and our
compute accessible to local builders." The concern becomes the *deal
structure*, not the *frame*. That's how you keep the toughness while
changing the posture.

One useful piece of real-world cover: this isn't theoretical. NYC's "AI
Advantage" plan, New Jersey's economic-development framing of its AI Hub,
and Tulsa's tech-led strategy are all early versions of the offensive
posture — while the more visible national conversation
(workforce-disruption executive orders, the future-of-work roadmaps) is
still mostly defensive. So a governor or mayor adopting this is joining a
live, credible trend, not inventing something unproven.

A quick note on the mayor/governor split, since they need slightly
different versions: *mayors* have the stronger hand on the "government that
works" and "local industry application" frames because they control service
delivery and know their economic base intimately — their story is concrete
and felt. *Governors* own the energy/grid, workforce systems, university
research, and regulatory leverage, so the "abundance forcing function" and
"our workers wield AI" frames are more theirs to drive. The
compute-as-productive-infrastructure pillar belongs to both.

If it's useful, I can take the next step in a couple of directions: draft
the actual narrative arc as it would sound in a State of the State / State
of the City address (the villain-stakes-vision-role-for-resident structure
that these speeches run on), or build out one pillar into a real policy
spine with the specific moves and proof points underneath it. Which would
help more — the rhetoric or the substance?

Illinois Hit Pause on Data Centers. The Smarter Move Is to Drive a Harder
Bargain.

*By Shaina Doar and Justin Massa*

That was fast.

Five years ago, Illinois courted data centers the way it would chase any
economic-development prize — a tax credit here, a sales-tax exemption
there, a ribbon cutting for the cameras. Between 2020 and 2024, 27 data
center projects collected more than $983 million in state tax benefits. We
were hardly alone. Nationally, 16 states handed out close to $6 billion in
sales-tax exemptions over five years, and that figure only counts the
states that bothered to disclose the number.

Then the mood turned. On July 1, Gov. JB Pritzker's pause on the state's
data center tax credit takes effect. New York's legislature just passed the
nation's first statewide moratorium. Ohio's governor hit the brakes on his
state's exemption. By this spring, fourteen states were weighing
moratoriums, more than 300 data center bills had been filed across
thirty-plus states, Bernie Sanders was calling for a national construction
freeze, and residents in Joliet were suing to stop a 795-acre project down
the road. We went from rolling out the welcome mat to threatening to roll
it back up — in about eighteen months.

The data center has become the physical manifestation of a much broader
anxiety about AI: a fifty-acre, humming, water-hungry symbol of a
technology most people never asked for and don't feel they control. Much
has been written about that backlash. Some of it is well founded. Some of
it isn't. But the building is where the abstraction becomes a neighbor —
and a line item on a utility bill.

I'm not here to relitigate the merits of AI. Yes, there will be dramatic
shifts in employment. Yes, whole industries will rise and fall. Yes, a
handful of people will get extraordinarily wealthy while most families keep
getting squeezed — on housing, on groceries, and now on power. Data centers
in the PJM grid that serves northern Illinois already drove roughly $9.3
billion in added capacity costs in a single auction, and analysts expect
them to push average U.S. electricity bills up around 8% by the end of the
decade. Those concerns are real, and they deserve real answers, not slogans.

But here is what almost no political or civic leader is saying out loud: AI
also has enormous power to help the very residents who are most anxious
about it. And I have yet to meet the mayor or governor who has truly
grabbed that opportunity by the horns. So let me suggest what it might look
like.

*What if Illinois used AI to fix the slow, opaque machinery of government
itself?* In most major metros, the median permitting timeline now
approaches two years. It does not have to. After January's wildfires,
California stood up an AI tool to fast-track rebuilding permits in Los
Angeles. Pennsylvania's environmental agency cleared a backlog of more than
2,400 permit applications down to zero last year. Louisville hired a chief
AI officer to do the same kind of work. Now imagine every Illinois
regulation digitized and searchable, each rule tied to its own policy
history and precedent — permits, licenses, and public benefits available
online, in plain language, in hours instead of months. That isn't dystopia.
That's a government that finally works at the speed its residents live at.

*What if we put AI to work for Main Street?* Small businesses that have
adopted generative AI are reporting striking results: four in five say
productivity is up 20% or more, 41% report revenue growth of 20% or more —
and 95% of regular users are *not* cutting headcount. That last number is
the whole game. The risk to the corner accounting firm or the neighborhood
manufacturer isn't that they adopt AI; it's that they don't, and get
flattened by an AI-first competitor. The right help at the right moment
keeps those businesses — and their jobs — alive.

*And what if we trained for the economy we're actually entering?* Not with
a one-off workshop, but with AI fluency built into every community college
program in the state, so a medical assistant, a welder, and a logistics
coordinator all graduate knowing how to work alongside these tools rather
than fearing them.

Here's the part the moratorium crowd is missing: data center demand has
never been higher, and the leverage has never been greater. The five
largest technology companies poured more than $400 billion into this
buildout in 2025 and are on track to spend roughly 75% more in 2026.
Goldman Sachs expects U.S. data center power demand to more than double by
2027. This is the currency of the next generation of technological change —
and these companies need somewhere to build. So why are our leaders either
quietly rolling over for the investment or loudly slamming the door? Both
are a failure of imagination. We are still negotiating as if this were a
Community Benefit Agreement over a new stadium.

Smart leaders will do something bolder. They will roll out the red carpet —
especially for the hyperscalers — in exchange for a real, binding
investment in transforming their economy and workforce for the AI era. The
ask should be explicit:

   - *Build the state's digital backbone.* Fund the digitization of all of
   Illinois' regulation, with traceable policy and precedent attached to each
   rule, and digital access to permits, approvals, and benefits for every
   resident and business.
   - *Train the workforce.* Underwrite AI training in every community
   college program, with subsidized degrees and credentials targeted at the
   jobs that will actually exist.
   - *Stand up an AI Corps.* Embed AI experts with small and mid-sized
   businesses so they can compete with AI-first entrants — raising output
   without shedding employees.
   - *Power it cleanly and modernize the grid.* Pay for the clean
   generation required to run these facilities, and use that same investment
   to harden and upgrade a grid that everyone depends on.
   - *Build it union.* Project labor agreements for the construction of the
   data centers themselves.

This is not a handout, and it is not a hostage negotiation. It is a trade —
one that turns the single largest private capital wave of our lifetimes
into digital infrastructure, skills, small-business resilience, and a
cleaner grid that outlast the buildings themselves.

A moratorium buys time. It does not build a future. Illinois has a brief,
closing window to set the terms before the capital flows somewhere else and
we are left with neither the investment nor the transformation. Our leaders
should stop choosing between welcoming data centers and fearing them — and
start asking them for something worth saying yes to.
------------------------------

*Shaina Doar is the Founder and Managing Director of Onward Ventures, an

has two decades of experience in economic development, real estate,
construction, and technology, including time at Google, the City of
Chicago, and McKinsey.*
