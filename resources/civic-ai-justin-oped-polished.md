# There Is Another Way to Bring AI to Your City

*Op-ed, polished draft (rewriter pass). Co-authored: Shaina Doar (Onward Ventures) and Justin Massa (Remix Partners).*

*Alternate headlines: (2) AI Will Reshape Your City's Economy. Who Captures the Value Is Still a Choice. (3) The AI Question Your City Isn't Being Asked.*

---

Ask almost any mayor, governor, or economic development director about artificial intelligence today, and you will hear one of two answers. The first is about regulation: which task force to convene, which risks to contain. The second is about procurement: which platform to standardize on, which "family" to join.

Both answers are reasonable. Both are also incomplete, and it is worth asking why. The people supplying the dominant story about AI are, for the most part, the people selling it. They are not lying. But they have a stake in your conclusion, and that stake quietly shapes the questions you are encouraged to ask. "How do we adopt this safely?" is a very different question from "who captures the value this creates?" The first keeps you a customer. The second makes you a strategist.

The second question is the one that matters, because AI will create real economic value, and value always accrues to someone. Right now, an enormous amount of money is being spent to make sure it accrues to the platforms rather than to your community. The deals being struck are built to turn what should be a swappable commodity into something with steep switching costs. The result is predictable. Value created inside your city's businesses flows out of your city.

The paradox sits in plain sight. The investment pouring into AI is aimed almost entirely at large enterprises, and when you ask large employers what AI will do to their workforce, the bigger they are, the more likely they are to say it will let them cut jobs. Ask small businesses the same question and the answer flips. According to a 2025 report from the U.S. Small Business Administration's Office of Advocacy, firms with fewer than twenty employees are the only ones that, on balance, expect AI to grow their headcount rather than shrink it. They are also the ones nobody is helping. The same study found that 82 percent of the smallest firms say AI is simply "not applicable" to them.

Put those two facts together and the stakes for any city come into focus. Small and mid-sized businesses are roughly half of local employment. They are the economic base. They are also the segment most likely to add jobs because of AI, and the segment receiving almost none of the help. The large employers who are getting that help expect to use it to employ fewer people. Left on its current track, AI hands your biggest companies a tool to shrink their payrolls and erode your tax base, while the businesses that would actually hire are left to work it out alone. The city absorbs the cost and misses the upside.

None of this is inevitable. It is the product of choices, and a city can make different ones.

This is why "regulate it" misses the point. Regulation is reactive. It tells you what to prevent, not what to build. The sharper question for a public leader is not how to referee AI but how to harness it. Given our industry mix, where are we vulnerable and where can we grow? Given our residents' skills, where do we invest in training, from kindergarten through adults already in the workforce? What happens to downtown if professional-services offices empty out, and how do we get ahead of that instead of scrambling after it? These are economic development questions. They have been the work of cities for a century. AI does not change the job. It raises the stakes.

The tools to act well are now within reach, and not only for the wealthiest places. The cost of capable AI is falling even as its capability climbs. A public library could soon offer what sits behind a corporate subscription today, for little more than the price of the electricity to run it. In most governments the bottleneck is procurement, not technology, and procurement is something leaders can fix. The cities that move deliberately, that keep their options open rather than lock themselves to a single provider, that treat AI as a question of local prosperity rather than a compliance exercise, will capture value their neighbors let slip away.

So here is the alternative we would put in place of the task force and the platform. Bring AI into civic life in a way that follows the evidence rather than a vendor's roadmap, that keeps value local rather than exporting it, and that starts from the interests of residents and small businesses. This is not a brake on innovation. It is how you get the good version of it. It is a bet that communities, given the full picture and a little help, will make better decisions for themselves than anyone will make for them.

The story your city is being told about AI is not wrong, exactly. It is incomplete, told by people with a reason to leave parts out, and the parts they leave out are the ones that decide who benefits. There is another way to do this, and we think it is the one worth choosing. The only real mistake would be to assume there isn't, and to let someone else make the choice for you.
